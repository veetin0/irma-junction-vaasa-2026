Irma · how the numbers are made

How Irma weighs evidence

Every percentage on Irma's overview comes from a short chain of simple arithmetic. This page walks through that chain with real examples from the data of 3 October 2026 (the demonstration clock), and says plainly which parts are standard methods and which parts are our own choices.

In short
  • A signal's weight is four conditions multiplied together: is the claim credible (publisher, further sources, doubt), is it current, is the move big, and what does the planner think. The weight can never exceed 1.
  • The weight is shown with a band (what it would be if the four conditions were combined in other ways), an attribution (which condition costs the most), and a fit to the time horizon (1, 2 and 3 years).
  • Each weighted signal pushes the situations it fits up and the ones it contradicts down. The pushes are added up and turned into percentages that always total 100%. The leading situation then decides which recommended move is shown.
  • The building blocks are well-known methods. The way they are combined and the numbers inside them are our own judgement, not an official standard, and they have not yet been checked against ABB's actual orders.

How much one signal counts

Irma asks four questions about every signal. Each answer becomes a number between 0 and 1 (the planner's view can go a little above 1), and the four numbers are multiplied. The result is the signal's weight, from 0 (ignore it) to 1 (it counts fully).

Why multiply, and why average?

The rule is simple: multiply conditions that are different things, and average estimators of the same thing. Whether a claim is true, current, big and undisputed are four different conditions that must all hold, so they are multiplied: the weakest link decides. A huge number from an unreliable blog should count little, and so should a solid fact that is two years old. If the four answers were added instead, a top score on one question could hide a failing score on another. When there are several ways to estimate the same thing, such as the size of a move, they are averaged with equal weights, because there is no history of outcomes to tell us which estimator deserves more.

One safety rule

Until Irma has searched for counter-evidence, a signal's weight is capped at 0.6. A claim that nobody has examined can never dominate the picture.

Strong and weak

A weight of 0.25 or more is listed as a strong signal, anything lower as weak. Today 11 of the 37 signals are strong. The automatic search adds a new signal only if its weight still reaches 0.15 after all its checks.

More than one source

When two publishers that do not copy each other report the same fact, the claim is more likely to be true. Irma combines independent sources in the same way it combines doubts: each source removes part of what is still unexplained. Syndicated copies of one press release count once, so a source on the same website or marked as derived from another is ignored.

The size of the move: several estimators

The size of a move is set when the signal is read, on a scale from 0 to 1. For signals with a long, sourced history, Irma adds a second estimate: how unusual the latest change is compared with the earlier ones (a robust statistical score). The two are averaged. Demonstration data never drives this estimate. Today no card has a sourced history long enough, so the size is the one set when the signal is read, and every weight equals what the earlier one-source formula gave.

A weight never goes above 1

The planner's view used to be able to push a weight out of range: a fresh, grade-A, full-strength card that is confirmed twice multiplies to 1 × 1 × 1 × 1.2 × 1.2 = 1.44. Irma now holds the weight at 1.00, and the horizon weights below can never exceed it.

The band: how much the recipe matters

Multiplying is a choice. Irma therefore also shows what the weight would be if the same four numbers were combined in other ways: the smallest of them, their geometric mean and their ordinary average. The spread is the uncertainty that comes from the recipe itself. The multiplied weight, which is the one used, is the strictest.

Where the discount comes from

A weight of 0.15 is far below 1. Irma shows which of the four conditions is responsible. The share of each is its part of the total discount from 1.0, measured in logarithms so that the order of the conditions does not matter, and the shares add up to 100%.

Step 2 of 4

How far ahead a signal speaks

Every card states how many months before an ABB order it becomes visible (its lead time), and whether its effect is structural, cyclical or mixed. Irma uses both to give each signal a weight for 12, 24 and 36 months. Before the effect has arrived inside a horizon, the signal counts for less, smoothly: a card that leads by 30 months says little about the next 12 months. After it has arrived, a structural signal keeps its full weight, a cyclical one fades (a price says little about two years later), and a mixed one sits halfway. The result never exceeds the signal's headline weight.

Read the later horizons as ranges

The width of the timing curve is our assumption (the typical spread between the low and high ends of the lead times on the cards is a factor of 2). With a narrower curve the 24-month leader switches to the Nordic situation, and with a wider one the 36-month leader switches back to "Growth continues, components stay scarce". The 12-month ranking does not depend on it.

Step 3 of 4

How a signal pushes the situations

Every signal is described by six themes: demand, component supply, rules and policy, material prices, a shift toward the Nordics, and stock-building by middlemen. Each of the nine candidate situations has its own pattern on the same six themes. The better the two patterns agree, the harder the signal pushes that situation up. If they point in opposite directions, it pushes the situation down. Each theme is scored from −1 to +1. Plus means more demand, tighter components, friendlier rules, dearer materials, a shift toward the Nordics, or middlemen stockpiling.

Irma compares the two patterns theme by theme. Demand agrees strongly (0.9 × 1.0) and the Nordic shift agrees a little (0.9 × 0.3). Adding these gives 1.17, and dividing by the size of the situation's own pattern (2.6) gives a match of 0.45. The match always lands between −1 and +1. The push is then 1.5 × weight × match: 1.5 × 0.59 × 0.45 = +0.40. The 1.5 is a fixed setting that limits how far one signal can move the picture. When a horizon is chosen, the weight at that horizon is used.

From the leading situation to a recommended move

The leading situation also decides the recommended move shown beside the overview. It is not written by a language model: it is put together by code from labelled templates. Each of the nine situations has three or four conditional moves for procurement, manufacturing, capacity and supply reliability, each tagged no-regret (worth doing in every plausible case) or contingent (only while its trigger holds), with the signals that would trigger it, the cost if it turns out wrong, and a horizon. The confidence comes from the gap to the runner-up and from the spread of the percentages: today the gap is 13 points and the spread 0.77, which gives medium. A hedge says what changes if the runner-up overtakes, and one next action names an owner and a due date. The move contains no quantities, order points or order dates: the order book lags the market by 18 months or more, and ABB's contracts, allocations and cash are not in open data. The planner decides.

Standard methods or our own invention?

Both. Each building block is an established, well-understood method. The recipe that combines them, and the numbers inside it, are our own choices for this prototype.

What this means for reading the percentages

Irma's percentages are a transparent ranking of today's evidence, not measured probabilities. Every number can be opened and traced to its sources, but nobody has yet checked how often a situation shown at 50% actually happens. Until that check is done, read 50% as "today's evidence points this way most strongly", not as "a 50% chance". The recommended move inherits this: it is conditional on the leading situation, says what would change it, and leaves quantities and dates to the planner.

How the numbers can be checked and tuned

  1. Keep the percentages over time. Irma already stores one point per day.
  2. After 6 to 18 months, compare them with what happened: ABB's order intake by segment and which situation actually unfolded.
  3. Score the past percentages with a standard accuracy measure for probability forecasts, the Brier score.
  4. Adjust the numbers we chose (grades, half-lives, the timing width, the setting 1.5, starting beliefs) to the values that would have scored best, and repeat.
Figures come from Irma's data for the current world situation on 3 October 2026 (the demonstration clock) and are rounded. The formulas are in the app's engine files weights.py, archetypes.py and recommend.py. Irma proposes conditional moves and a next action; it gives no order quantities and no order timing, and the planner decides.
